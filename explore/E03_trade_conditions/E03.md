## Part A — forward returns of the cheaper and the richer leg, by z-score

- **z** is E01's: (plain ratio − mean of the last 52 weekly closes) ÷ their sd, T02's band in units of its own sd.
- **The cheaper leg** is the one that has lagged: leg a when z < 0 (the ratio a ÷ b is below its mean), leg b when z > 0.
  The other is the richer leg. This is also the leg the T01/T02 rule holds once |z| > 1.
- **Buckets:** z < −2, −2 to −1, −1 to 0, 0 to 1, 1 to 2, > 2 — and all weeks together.
- **Forward returns:** from the decision week's close to the close 4, 13 and 26 weeks later: the cheaper leg's, the
  richer leg's, their difference (cheaper − richer, the "spread": what the rule earns gross of costs if it holds the
  cheaper leg), and the average of the two (what the market did). Simple returns, no costs, no fills.
- **Per bucket and horizon:** the number of weeks, the number of episodes (runs of consecutive weeks in the bucket —
  the forward windows overlap, so the episodes are the nearer count of independent observations), the mean and the
  median of each return.
- **Against the random walks:** E01's 2,000 random paths of the ratio per pair (the same signs, seed
  `default_rng([101, pair_id])`; the point-in-time residuals with random signs plus the expanding drift, after the real
  52-week warm-up). The two legs' *common* movement is kept real: with M = ½(ln a + ln b), the random legs are
  a* = exp(M + ½ ln r*) and b* = exp(M − ½ ln r*), so only their relative movement is random. The same statistics on
  every path; percentile = (paths below + ½ ties) ÷ paths; notable = ≤ 5 or ≥ 95. A random walk's spread has no
  reason to depend on z, so a notable spread in the stretched buckets is the only kind of pattern the rule could use.
- **Point-in-time:** z at week t uses weeks up to t. The forward returns are, by construction, the future from t; the
  profile describes the history, it is not a signal (its buckets were chosen after T01 and T02 were locked, but the
  rule's own band, ±1, was fixed before either).

## Part B — the market's condition at each trade, and how the trade did

- **Trades:** every closed round trip of T01 and of T02 at window 52, exit E1, 20.8 bps (open trades are left out).
  **Outcome:** the per-trade "extra" of T01 §4.3 (the held leg's fill-to-fill return minus the peer's, no cost), the
  held leg's own return, the weeks held, the exit reason (back at the mean, or the 26-week cap). **Good** = extra > 0.
- **Conditions at entry** (at the entry decision's close, from data up to that day only; checked by recomputing every
  condition series from the history cut at 12 dates per pair, counted in audit.json):
  1. *Correlation between the legs:* trailing 26- and 52-week Pearson correlation of the legs' weekly log returns.
  2. *Market regime:* N100's trailing 52-week return (N100 is the market for every pair, gold pairs included) and its
     drawdown from its highest close so far.
  3. *Volatility and band width:* the held leg's and the peer's trailing 26-week volatility (annualised), the ratio's,
     and the band's relative width (2 × sd ÷ mean of the trailing 52 weekly ratios, in %).
  4. *Entry depth and speed:* |z| at entry; weeks since z last had the other sign; the held leg's return minus the
     peer's over the 4 and 13 weeks before entry (how fast it lagged); each leg's own 13-week return; the share of the
     ten ratios outside their own band that week (breadth); the calendar month (descriptive only).
- **Conditions during the trade** (concurrent — knowable only afterwards, labelled so): N100's return from entry fill
  to exit fill; the legs' correlation over the weeks held (4 weeks or more, else blank); the ratio's volatility over them.
- **Pools:** the six index pairs together and the four gold pairs together, for each test. Within each pool the trades
  are ranked within their pair before pooling, so a pair's level (a gold pair's volatility, say) does not drive the
  pooled number.
- **Two statistics per condition, test and pool:** (i) the median of the condition among good trades minus among bad
  trades; (ii) the rank correlation (Spearman) between the condition and the extra.
- **Null:** permutation. The extras (or the good/bad labels) are shuffled among the trades *within each pair*, 2,000
  times, seed `default_rng([105, test_id, pool_id])`; percentile of the real statistic among the shuffled ones; notable
  = ≤ 5 or ≥ 95 (a two-sided 10% rate by chance). The null asks only "is this condition associated with the outcome?";
  it says nothing about whether a filtered rule would beat random timing — that is a new test.
- **Multiplicity, stated up front:** about 20 conditions × 2 statistics × 2 tests × 2 pools ≈ 160 percentiles, so
  about 16 are expected to be notable by chance; the page counts them. T01's and T02's trades overlap heavily (the same
  band on nearly the same series), so the two tests are *not* two replications; the script reports the overlap.
- **Also shown:** the trades on a timeline over the two legs (rebased), good and bad coloured, per pair and test; the
  extra split by exit reason (the question of T01 §7, row 1, here only described).

## Added after the run (2026-11-17)

Nothing above was changed. Every number is in `out/E03_results.md` and on the page, printed by the script; the notes below
say how to read them.

1. **"Good" is almost the same as "exited at the mean".** Nearly every trade that exits at the mean has a positive extra
   and nearly every trade that runs to the 26-week cap has a negative one (the exit-reason table). So Part B mostly asks
   which conditions go with the ratio *getting back to its mean within 26 weeks*. The capped trades also carry the large
   losses (the capped trades' mean extra is two to three times the size of the mean-exit trades', with the opposite sign),
   which is where T01's and T02's negative mean extra comes from.
2. **The "during" conditions are partly a restatement of the outcome.** A trade that runs to the cap is one in which the
   ratio kept moving the wrong way, so the legs diverged (lower correlation) and the ratio was more volatile. Those
   associations describe what a bad trade looks like; they do not show what to look for at entry.
3. **The conditions at entry that line up with good trades are about speed and depth, not the market or correlation.**
   For the index pairs, in both tests: fewer weeks since z crossed zero, a sharper lag of the held leg in the 4 weeks
   before entry, and a larger |z| go with better trades; the legs' correlation at entry and the market's 52-week return
   do not. For the gold pairs, a higher 26-week correlation at entry and a deeper market drawdown go with better trades.
   These are candidates, not findings: Part B has 144 percentiles against about 14 notable by chance, and T01 and T02 are
   one sample seen twice (the overlap line), so a condition notable in both tests is one observation, not two.
4. **Six T01 trades enter with the plain z just inside ±1** (|z| 0.97–1.00); all of T02's enter beyond ±1. T01's band is
   on the log ratio, so on the plain z its entries can fall a hair inside; this is the same near-identity of the two
   forms that T02 records as F-RA-M02.
5. **Part A: the cheaper leg does not catch up more than a random walk's would.** Where the spread differs from the
   random walks in the stretched buckets, it is mostly lower (blue), not higher. SC100 ÷ Micro250's red cells inherit
   E01's caveat for that pair: its random walks carry the steep drift of its 2022 warm-up year.
6. **The independent review agents did not run** (a spend limit). The spot checks done instead recomputed one Part A
   cell with pandas (equal), matched all 195 T02 extras with the evidence file (0 difference), checked that the held leg
   is the cheaper one on every trade, recomputed one 52-week correlation by hand (equal) and checked the permutation
   draws; all agreed.
