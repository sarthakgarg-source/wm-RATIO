# E01 — Is there cyclicality in the ratio series? (EXPLORATORY)

**Status:** EXPLORATORY — description, not a finding. No trading rule is tested here, nothing here gets a finding ID, and no
number from it may be quoted as evidence for a rule (RESEARCH_PLAYBOOK.md Part 3). Disclosed in T02.md §2 because it
looks at the pair ratios before T02 is locked.
**Asked:** 2026-11-13 — "i want to viualise the data and chech for cyclicality … in the ratio series"
**Script:** `python explore/E01_ratio_cycles/cycles.py` → `explore/E01_ratio_cycles/out/` (page E01_cycles.html, E01_results.md, CSVs, audit.json)
**Data:** the approved data of 2026-11-12, only through `analysis/approved_data.load_daily()`, on each pair's common
calendar and decision weeks exactly as T02/T03 build them (`analysis.ratio_engine.pair_frame`): index pairs from
2006-01-02, gold pairs from 2015-01-12, Micro250 pairs from 2022-01-10, all to 2026-08-28.

## The series

For each of the 10 pairs: the plain ratio r_t = P_a ÷ P_b on the last trading day of each ISO week (T02's series), and its
weekly change g_t = ln(r_t ÷ r_{t−1}) — the ratio's week-on-week change in per cent (continuously compounded). The weekly
change is the same whether the ratio is drawn plain or logged; the log/plain choice matters only for where a band sits,
not for cycles in the ratio. Before any statistic the average weekly change (the ratio's long-run drift) is
taken out: z_t = g_t − mean(g).

## The random-walk comparison (the null)

The question each analysis asks is "does the ratio do this more than a random walk would?" A random walk with the same
volatility is built by **flipping the sign of each week's change at random** (z*_t = s_t × z_t, s_t = ±1 with equal odds):
every week keeps its size, so volatility and its clustering (2008, 2020) are kept, but any link between one week and the
next — momentum, mean reversion, a cycle — is destroyed. 2,000 such paths per pair; seed `default_rng([101, pair_id])`
(pair_id = the pair's position in the engine's PAIRS list). Every statistic is computed the same way on the real series and
on each path; the result is the real series' percentile among the 2,000 (percentile = (paths below + ½ ties) ÷ paths ×
100). **Notable** = at or below the 5th or at or above the 95th percentile (outside the random walks' middle 90%).

## The four checks

1. **Multi-year (and shorter) cycles — the spectrum.** The periodogram of z shows how much of the ratio's weekly variance
   sits at each cycle length. A random walk's is flat; a cycle of period P shows as excess power near P; mean reversion
   at a horizon shows as a deficit at longer periods, trending as an excess. Power is summed in period bands of 2–4, 4–8,
   8–13, 13–26, 26–52, 52–104, 104–208 and 208–520 weeks, as a share of total variance. Only cycle lengths that fit at
   least 3 times in the pair's window are used (periods ≤ weeks ÷ 3: about 7 years for the index pairs, under 4 for the
   gold pairs, 1½ for the Micro250 pairs). One overall test per pair, so that trying 8 bands is not 8 chances: the largest
   |log(power ÷ the random walks' median power)| over the bands, against the same maximum in each random path.
   *No moving-average detrending anywhere*: smoothing a random walk with a moving average manufactures cycles of about the
   window's length (the Slutsky–Yule effect), which would make a 52-week band look cyclical by construction.
2. **Mean reversion by horizon — variance ratios.** VR(k) = variance of k-week changes ÷ (k × variance of 1-week changes),
   overlapping windows, for k = 2, 4, 8, 13, 26, 52, 104 weeks (only k ≤ weeks ÷ 4). Below 1: moves over k weeks tend to
   undo themselves (reversion, the swing-back the ±1 sd band bets on); above 1: they tend to continue (trend).
3. **Calendar seasonality.** The ratio's change over each calendar month (last common trading day to last common trading
   day), averaged by month of the year. Null: within each calendar year the twelve monthly changes are shuffled among the
   months (keeps every year's total; destroys any month-of-year pattern); 2,000 shuffles; seed `default_rng([104, pair_id])`.
   Overall test: the spread (sd) of the 12 monthly averages. Only pairs with at least 8 years in every month are tested
   (the Micro250 pairs, about 4½ years, are shown but not tested).
4. **Swing durations.** On the drift-removed ratio, a turning point is confirmed when the ratio reverses by at least one
   typical half-year move, h = sd(z) × √26, from its last extreme (a zig-zag filter; h is set per pair from its own
   volatility and applied unchanged to every random path). Measured: the number of completed swings, their median
   length in weeks, and how regular they are (coefficient of variation of the lengths). A real cycle would show as swings
   more regular than a random walk's (low CV); reversion as more, shorter swings; trending as fewer, longer ones.

## How to read it, and its limits

- About 10% of the percentiles will be notable by chance alone if every ratio were a random walk; the page counts how
  many are notable against that expectation. One notable cell is not a cycle.
- 20 years hold only ~3–7 repetitions of a 3–7 year cycle; the spectrum at those lengths is coarse, whatever it shows.
- The pairs share legs (N100 is in four), so their results are not independent.
- Gold: front-month futures continuation with an unconfirmed roll on 2026-01-30 (F-RA-D-OPEN-2); gold pairs from 2015.
- Price indices without dividends; both legs alike, so the ratio's cycles are unaffected except through the dividend gap.
- Anything that looks like a usable cycle becomes a pre-registered test of its own (a new T number) before it is believed.

## Added after the run (2026-11-13)

Everything above was written before the first run and is unchanged; the method it describes is still the one the page's
main charts use.

1. **The drift removal uses hindsight.** z_t = g_t − mean(g) takes the mean of the *whole* 2006–2026 history, so the
   drift-removed series at any week uses information from after it; the zig-zag threshold h = sd(z) × √26 is likewise a
   whole-history sd. This is not point-in-time. It is acceptable for a description of the history — no decision is made
   at any date, and every random walk is demeaned and thresholded the same way, so the treatment cannot favour the real
   ratio over them — but it was checked rather than assumed.
2. **The check.** `cycles.py` now also computes every statistic the drift removal can touch with **no drift removal**
   (B): the real ratio's raw weekly changes, against the same sign-flipped random walks with the drift added back (same
   signs, same seeds). The spectrum is identical by construction (a constant only touches the zero frequency, which is
   never used) and the month-of-year check never removed the drift, so B covers the variance ratios and the three swing
   measures. The comparison, and the count of statistics whose notable call changes, are printed by the script — in
   `out/E01_results.md` ("Does removing the drift with the whole history matter?"), `out/drift_check.csv`, `out/audit.json`
   (`drift_check`) and a section of the page — never typed here. The main results (A) are unchanged by the addition: the
   same signs and seeds give the same numbers.
3. **What it means for later work.** If anything here is turned into a rule, the rule must use only trailing information
   (a trailing drift and a trailing sd, or none), under a pre-registered test with counted look-ahead assertions; the
   zig-zag turning points cannot be traded as drawn (a peak is confirmed only after the ratio has fallen by 1 − e^−h).
4. **A wording fix.** In plain per cent the zig-zag threshold is not symmetric: a trough is confirmed by a rise of e^h − 1,
   a peak by a fall of 1 − e^−h (the same distance in log terms). Item 4 of "The four checks" says "reverses by at least
   … h", which is right in log terms; the page's methods now give both per-cent figures.
5. **Switched to point-in-time (expanding) estimates, at the desk's request, the same day.** Items 1–3 described the
   whole-history version and a check of it; that version is no longer on the page (it is in git: commit 9a535a2 holds the
   first run). Now the drift removed at week t is the mean of the weekly changes *before* t, the zig-zag threshold at t is
   the sd of those weeks × √26, and the first 52 weeks are a warm-up for both (not analysed themselves; the ratio charts
   still show them). The random walks are the point-in-time residuals with random signs and no demeaning. No
   whole-history estimate enters any series, threshold or random walk; the descriptive "drift a year" column is the only
   whole-window number and no calculation uses it. A look-ahead check now runs every time: each pair's history is cut at
   12 dates, everything is recomputed from the cut history and must equal the full run up to the cut, and the turning
   points found on the cut history must be the first ones of the full run — counted in `out/audit.json`
   (`point_in_time.look_ahead_check`), and the script stops on any violation. It was shown to catch a planted leak (a
   drift that peeks at the next week). The drift check in item 2 now compares expanding drift removal (A) with none (B),
   both point-in-time, spectrum included. This change came after the results were seen; it does not alter what any
   check measures, only how the drift and threshold are estimated.
6. **What the point-in-time version costs.** A drift estimated from earlier weeks starts from the warm-up year. Where that
   year differs sharply from what followed — the Micro250 pairs' warm-up is 2022 — removing it leaves a slow trend in the
   residuals that random walks do not have, which pushes the variance ratios towards "continues"; adding it to the random
   walks (B) pushes the other way. The script names the pairs where A and B disagree on more than one call; for them
   neither reading is reliable. The 20-year index pairs are barely affected.

## Added 2026-11-17: the z-score of the ratio (design written before it was run)

Asked: "i want to add z score of ratio in the analysis as well". Choices made with the desk: on this page only, the
trailing-52-week window, and description only.

- **Definition.** z_t = (r_t − mean of r over the last 52 weekly closes including week t) ÷ (their sd, n − 1), from
  the 42nd week on. This is exactly T02's band, so z = −1 and z = +1 are its lower and upper edges. Point-in-time by
  construction. Checked, not assumed: the z recomputed from the history cut at 12 dates must equal the full run's up to
  each cut (counted with the other look-ahead checks), and it must equal (ratio − mean) ÷ sd built from the band columns
  of the engine's own `decide()` (T02's plain form, window 52). Only those two columns are read; none of the engine's
  decisions, states or trades is read or reported.
- **Description only.** Nothing about what follows a band touch is computed: no return of the ratio, no time back to
  the mean, no hit rate. That is T02's own question and stays unseen until T02 is frozen and run.
- **What is shown, per pair.** The z series under the ratio chart, with the ±1 (the band) and ±2 lines and E01's turning
  points; the share of weeks beyond ±1 (above +1 and below −1 separately too) and beyond ±2; the stretches beyond ±1
  (consecutive weeks above +1, or below −1): how many there are, their median and longest length; the median z at E01's
  peaks and at its troughs; the lowest, highest and latest z with dates. Plus all ten pairs' z on one timeline.
- **Against random walks.** The share of weeks beyond ±1, the share beyond ±2, the median stretch length beyond ±1, the
  median z at peaks and the median z at troughs are each given a percentile among the page's own 2,000 random walks per
  pair (the same signs and seeds). The z is taken on the random path's *ratio*, so each random step is the residual with
  its random sign plus the expanding drift added back, as in the drift check's version B. A trailing z on a trending
  series sits on the trend's side, and the random paths then trend like the real ratio. Each random path starts from
  the real ratio's warm-up weeks, so its first z values use the same history. Turning points on a random path come
  from the zig-zag on its drift-removed path, as for the real ratio. The window is the page's: from the end of the
  52-week warm-up to the last week. A stretch running at either end of the window is cut there, on the real ratio and
  the random paths alike.
- **Counting.** The new percentiles join the page's count of notable percentiles against chance. They are not
  independent of the variance ratios: a ratio that reverses more than a random walk also spends less time far from its
  mean.
- **Outputs.** `out/zscores.csv` (per pair), `out/z_weekly.csv` (pair, date, ratio, trailing mean, sd, z), a section in
  `out/E01_results.md`, a column in the summary, a panel and table on the page, and `zscore` in `out/audit.json`.

### After the z-score run (2026-11-17)

Nothing in the design above changed. Every number is in `out/E01_results.md` and on the page, printed by the script.

1. **The checks passed and are counted.** z equals the engine's band, z is unchanged when the history is cut, and the
   random paths' method equals the real z on the real ratio. All three are in `out/audit.json` (`zscore.checks`,
   `point_in_time.look_ahead_check`). A planted one-week peek in the z (a window shifted one week ahead) was caught at
   all 12 cuts. Every result published before the z was added (every earlier column of every CSV) is unchanged.
2. **A reading aid the results make necessary.** A trailing 52-week z on a random walk is not a bell curve. The random
   walks are beyond ±1 in more than half their weeks and beyond ±2 in far more than one week in twenty, so neither is
   a rare event on these series. The page shows the random walks' figure next to every z share for that reason.
3. **The z comparison inherits item 6.** Its random walks are the drift check's version B. Where A and B disagree, the
   z comparison is not reliable either. The script names those pairs beside the z results, in the results file and on
   the page.
4. **The count against chance now includes the five z percentiles per pair**, so its totals differ from the count
   before the z was added. Both are generated, never typed.
