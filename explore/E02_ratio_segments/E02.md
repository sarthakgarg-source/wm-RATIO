## Why it is hindsight (stated, not hidden)

1. A peak's **prominence** is how far the series falls on each side before it meets higher ground — it needs the data
   after the peak.
2. The prominence **filter** compares each peak with the average prominence of *all* peaks in the window.
3. A kept peak survives the **local-maximum step** only if it is higher than the *next* kept peak.
4. Each **regression** spans the whole segment, start to end.

So the segments describe the history as it looks now. None of their dates was knowable at the time; nothing here can be
traded as drawn, and the page says so on every chart.

## The method, as the desk's code runs it

1. Rebase the series to 100 at its first day (it does not change where the peaks are).
2. `scipy.signal.find_peaks(x, prominence=0)`: every local maximum and its topographic prominence = height above the
   higher of the two lowest points reached on either side before higher ground (or the end of the series). Keep a peak if
   its prominence ≥ **m × the average prominence** of all peaks (code default m = 5).
3. Troughs: the same on the negated series.
4. Local-maximum step: of the kept peaks, keep each one that is higher than the kept peak before it and the one after it
   (so the first and last are dropped); troughs likewise, lower than both neighbours.
5. Merge and sort the surviving peaks and troughs; each consecutive pair bounds a **segment**.
6. A linear regression on each segment; classify **up** / **down** / **sideways** by its slope.
7. Merge a segment shorter than 30 rows with the neighbour whose trend is closer; then merge neighbours whose trends differ
   by no more than the merge threshold (code: 15°); repeat until nothing changes. The first and last segments are only
   ever merged as a neighbour (as in the code).

## The two changes, and why

- **A. The trend test is in % a year, not degrees.** The code classifies by the angle of the regression line on the
  rebased-to-100 series, up if steeper than +30° (slope 0.577 points a row). That is 145 points a year on daily data; a
  ratio never moves that fast, so in a probe on N100 ÷ SC100 every one of the 13 segments came out "sideways" (steepest
  12°). An angle in points also depends on the level the series has reached since the rebase. Here the regression is of
  ln(ratio) on time in years, so its slope is the segment's trend in % a year (e^slope − 1), the same at any level.
  Up if the trend > +T, down if < −T, sideways otherwise.
- **B. The merge threshold is in the same units:** neighbours merge when their trends differ by no more than T ÷ 2 (the
  code's 15° is half its 30°).

Everything else is as the code: the prominence filter, the local-maximum step, the minimum segment of 30 rows (30 trading
days ≈ 6 weeks), the merge order. Two harmless clean-ups: a segment's regression includes its end point (the code drops the
last row from the regression but keeps it in the segment), and a merged segment does not count the shared pivot day twice.
Kept as in the code, and reported: peaks and troughs need not alternate (two peaks can follow each other); the page counts
how often.

## The grid (shown whole, not tuned)

- prominence multiplier m = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 (5 = the code's default);
- trend threshold T = 5%, 10%, 20% a year (merge threshold T ÷ 2);
- minimum segment 30 trading days throughout.
- **Reference setting for the charts: m = 3, T = 10% a year** — the middle of each grid, chosen by position before the run.
- Also reported, for transparency: the code exactly as written (daily, m = 5, ±30°, merge 15°).

There is no ground truth to optimise against (no hand-labelled turning points for these ratios), so the grid is described,
not searched for a best setting.

## The cross-check against E01

E01's turning points are point-in-time (zig-zag on the weekly, drift-removed ratio). For each pair and setting: the share
of E02's peaks and troughs that have an E01 turning point of the same type within ±8 weeks, and the share of E01's turning
points that have an E02 one. Agreement says the two methods see the same big swings; disagreement is expected where E01's
threshold catches swings E02's prominence filter drops, and where E02 uses hindsight E01 cannot.

## Limits

- Hindsight throughout (above). The segment boundaries move when data is added at the end: the last segment and the
  prominence of recent peaks are provisional.
- The prominence filter is relative (m × the average of this pair's own peaks), so how many peaks survive depends on the
  window's length and on the mix of small and large wiggles in it.
- Regression classes depend on T; the grid shows how much.
- Micro250 pairs cover 2022–2026 only; the gold leg has an unconfirmed roll on 2026-01-30 (F-RA-D-OPEN-2).

## Added after the run (2026-11-16)

Nothing above was changed; two things the first run showed are now reported by the script rather than left implicit.

1. **A short first or last segment can show an extreme % a year.** The code never merges the first or last segment, so one
   shorter than the 30-day minimum can survive, and annualising a few weeks' move gives a number like +189% a year
   (N100 ÷ SC100's last segment). Each segment now carries its total move, turning point to turning point, beside the
   trend a year — in the tables, the chart's hover and `out/segments.csv`.
2. **A trend running at the end of the window can be left unclassified.** The local-maximum step keeps a peak only if it
   is higher than the kept peaks on both sides, so in a long steady trend every intermediate peak is dropped (and the last
   kept peak never qualifies). N100 ÷ MC100's last turning point is in 2022 although the ratio kept falling to 2026. The
   summary now shows, per pair, how long and how far the ratio has moved since its last turning point.
3. **Turning points next to the unconfirmed gold roll.** The gold pairs' last turning points fall on 2026-01-29, the day
   before the −12.4% gold print of 2026-01-30 that the data review left open (F-RA-D-OPEN-2). They may be data artefacts.
   The script now lists every turning point within 5 days of that date, in the results and on the page.
